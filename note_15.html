THE SPELL LEARNING CURVE

Subject: Intelligence scaling and its interaction with learning new spells.

Prerequisite: Read through Book 8 before opening these notes.

1. Attribute Scaling

Maximum possible attribute values expand exponentially by a power of six at each tier (6T).

Tier 0 1 2 3 4 5 6
Max Attribute 6 36 216 1,296 7,776 46,656 279,936

2. Baseline Learning Difficulty Formula

Current theory posits that spell learning difficulty scales in the same manner as attributes, offset by a minimum baseline attempt count that is linear with tier.

3. Intelligence Credit Mitigation

A credit is applied based on the Player's intelligence stat. Assuming a starting intelligence of 3 at Tier 0 with the standard automatic tripling at each Tier, a baseline Player maintains the following minimum intelligence values and actual needed attempt counts:

Tier 0 1 2 3 4 5 6
Base Intelligence 3 9 27 81 243 729 2187
Actual Player Attempts 9 39 207 1,239 7,563 45,963 277,791

*Field Observation: Field data shows new arrivals take between 18 and 24 tries to acquire their first Tier 0 spell. This is higher than our calculated 9 tries. This difference is likely due to an oversimplification of the minimum required attempts. It is known that factors do affect the minimum, including having a trainer, only learning one spell at a time, and the precision of each attempt. Still the curve holds up. It cleanly demonstrates why so few Players ever reach Tier 4. --I. W.

4. Downward Tier Learning Efficiency

Intelligence directly reduces difficulty, but cannot reduce total tries below the tier's required minimum attempt threshold. When an established Tier 3 Player acquires lower-tier spells, difficulty drops dramatically:

Base Difficulty 6 36 216 1,296
Tier 3 Intelligence Credit 81 81 81 81
Actual Tries Required 6 12 153 1,239

5. How Does Anyone Reach Tier 4?

Relying solely on automatic tier-tripling leaves a Player facing 1,239 tries to master a Tier 3 control. The key to reaching higher tiers lies in pushing intelligence far beyond baseline doubling at tier.

Intelligence stat growth is driven by diversity in magic. Learning as many skill and roles as possible will increase a Player's intelligence stat beyond the doubling at tier thresholds.

  • Multiple Control Trees: Studying across Light, Sound, Force, Temperature, Electricity, Chemistry, and Gravitonics.
  • Multiple Schools: Cast, Imbuing, Crafting, Chemistry, Group, Enchanting, Song.
  • Role Adaptability: Transitioning between Scout, Anchor, Healer, Tactician, Amplifier, Striker, and Disruptor.
  • Weapon Diversity: Mastering Staff, Hammer, Axe, Spear, Sword, Knife, and Bow configurations.
  • Utility Variance: Cloaking, Enhancement, Defensive, Offensive, Imbuing, and Recycling spell types.

TIME INVESTMENT ANALYSIS (TIER 3 -> TIER 4):
Advancing to Tier 4 requires mastering twelve Tier 3 spells. At an optimized rate of 154 attempts per spell and 5 minutes per attempt, learning 12 spells takes 154 total hours (or roughly 19.25 eight-hour training days).

In practice, native-born residents average a minimum of 10 years to bridge this gap due to resource constraints, combat fatigue, and failure to sufficiently cross-train.

*Note on Tier 6: Although mathematical formulas exist for Tier 6 data, no verified Tier 6 spells have been recorded in colonial history. --I. W.

6. Learning Specific Spells

The previous analysis assumes that all control codes within a tier require equal training effort. In practice, significant structural variances exist based on code architecture and spell nature. Higher-tier codes generally fall into four structural classifications:

Spell Architecture & Difficulty Hierarchy

1. Combination Codes (Lowest Difficulty):
Combines two or more lower-tier codes. If all prerequisite lower-tier codes are already mastered, the number of required attempts is minimal. Combination requires only half the number of attemts to learn compared to what is required for new functionality. Usually, only one primary prerequisite is hard-required by the system; however, if any secondary prerequisite codes remain unlearned, the attempt counts needed to master those missing prerequisites are added directly onto the higher-tier code's learning threshold.

2. Amplified / Stronger Versions (Moderate Difficulty):
A direct tier-upgrade to an existing prerequisite. Because the lower-tier code is a hard prerequisite, no missing-prerequisite attempt penalties are added. Furthermore, the there is no combination overhead.

3. Amplified / Added Functionality (Elevated Difficulty):
A direct tier-upgrade to an existing prerequisite. Because the lower-tier code is a hard prerequisite, no missing-prerequisite attempt penalties are added. The additional functionality has a cost similar to a single missed-prerequisite, plus the required attempts for the combination.

4. Standalone / No-Prerequisite Codes (Highest Difficulty):
New control codes requiring no antecedent knowledge. These incur the full base learning cost of the function, the combination overhead, plus an additional attempt penalty calculated as if the Player were missing one prerequisite code across all utilized control trees.

7. The Destructive Potential Penalty

Beyond structural code complexity, the Game applies a safety modifier based on potential interference with other players' game play.

FIELD COMPARISON // DESTRUCTIVE MULTIPLIERS:
Consider two Tier 5 spells:
  • Conceal (Tier 5 Cloaking): Relatively easy to master due to non-lethal, utility-focused mechanics.
  • Ring of Death (Tier 5 Offensive): Possesses the raw system output capacity to eliminate over a thousand Players in a single invocation.
To prevent accidental catastrophic misuse, the Structure doubles the total attempt count required to learn high-fatality spells like Ring of Death.
